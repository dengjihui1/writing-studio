# Drafting workflow

## 1. Reader promise

Write one sentence answering: after reading, what should the reader understand, believe, feel, or do? Use it as a decision rule, not necessarily as published prose.

## 2. Material inventory

Separate available material into:

- claims or ideas;
- evidence, examples, data, quotations, or scenes;
- constraints and required inclusions;
- missing support;
- language or terminology that must remain stable.

Do not treat a claim as evidence for itself.

## 3. Structural spine

Create the smallest outline that can deliver the promise. Give each section a role such as context, problem, thesis, explanation, evidence, complication, implication, decision, or close.

For each section, check:

- What question does it answer?
- What new work does it perform?
- What support makes it credible?
- Why must it appear here?

Remove sections that only repeat, decorate, or delay.

For an evidence-led essay or report, consider a case spine: one recurring case, decision, process, or dispute that can carry the central question through more than one section. Use other sources where they explain, challenge, or extend that spine. Do not turn the paper into a catalogue in which every available topic receives equal treatment.

Allocate attention before drafting. Give more space to evidence that changes the thesis, contested mechanisms, and difficult boundaries; compress familiar definitions and secondary categories. Apparent completeness is not a substitute for judgement.

Give paragraphs local contracts as well. Record the question each paragraph answers, the evidence or example it uses, and what changes for the reader by the end. Adjacent contracts should not all repeat “claim, support, qualification, summary”. Alternate functions only when the argument requires it: a case may expose a problem, a mechanism may explain it, and a counterexample may narrow the next claim.

## 4. Drafting order

Draft the logical core first. Openings and titles are easier to write after the argument exists. Use concrete nouns and verbs, stable terminology, and explicit logical relations. Vary sentence length for rhythm without sacrificing clarity.

Do not draft from a stock introduction-body-conclusion cadence when the material suggests a more useful shape. Let paragraph length, sentence movement, and emphasis follow the work being done: explanation may need patience, evidence may need compression, and a turn in the argument may deserve a short sentence. Avoid drafting ornamental transitions or generic significance claims that will later need to be stripped out.

Keep placeholders visible when support is missing, for example: `[补充：受访者原话]` or `[verify current figure]`.

## 5. Reverse outline

After drafting, reduce each paragraph to one sentence describing its job. Then verify:

- every paragraph advances the reader promise;
- the first sentence makes the paragraph's role legible;
- support belongs to the claim it follows;
- transitions express a real relation rather than decorative linking;
- no conclusion is stronger than the available evidence.
- adjacent paragraphs cannot be swapped without damaging the argument;
- no long paragraph contains two independent setups or conclusions left by mechanical merging;
- citations perform different, identifiable functions rather than appearing in a repeated source-summary-limit pattern.

Repair structure before sentence polish.

After structural repair, run the human-writing pass in `human-writing.md`; do not use sentence variation to disguise an unresolved logical gap.

## 6. Openings and endings

An opening should establish relevance, orientation, and forward motion without generic scene-setting. In an academic essay, a specific case or disputed decision can establish the problem before the thesis is stated. An ending should deliver consequence, synthesis, decision, image, or next action—not repeat the introduction or inventory every section. A conclusion may return to the opening case if the intervening argument has changed what that case means.
